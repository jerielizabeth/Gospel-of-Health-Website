[Intro pp to ease into the rest.]

The process for creating a narrative dissertation in history, on the whole, well established. A researchers chooses a topic of study, a theoretical framework (or two), identifies the relevant archives, spends years in those archives reading and analyzing the materials, and then uses that evidence to construct an account of a particular time, place, person, or event that had some influence on the development of culture, of politics, or of institutions. Because this process has been well defined over the last hundred years of academic scholarship, there is generally little attention payed to the process, beyond the traditional acknowledgment in introductions and footnotes of the relevant theoretical frameworks and the consulted archival sources.

With the rise of digital technologies, many of the assumptions regarding the standard processes for archival research, analysis, and even publication have been open to challenge and revision. Where the standard methodology for interpretation was reading and the application of logic, computational algorithms enable new forms of analysis of data to ground interpretive claims.^[Tie in @Clement2016 to ground claim that methodology has always existed, just has been under theorized.] Additionally, the use of computers in the processing of more traditional forms of historical evidence raise important questions about how that research might be examined and reproduced. Where scholarly arguments based on interpretation could be evaluated through a rereading of the historical objects and a consideration of the logic of the initial interpretation, work that relies on computation requires engagement with the "source code" in order to be evaluated. A reader cannot adequately assess arguments where the author notes a source base and a general computational approach, as the part of the logic of the analysis is embedded in the particulars of the implementation and is only visible through the code.^[These concerns are not unique to history or the humanities. As computation and data science gain ground in the sciences as a mechanism for knowledge production, similar questions around reproducibility and code are increasingly of central concern.]

Additionally, the very process of using computational tools in the analysis of historical data and the crafting of the interpretive narrative expands what has traditionally been considered the work of an individual scholar into a more obvious composite of the intellectual work of multiple authors.^[Whether that myth of the individual author has ever been true is another question, as all intellectual work relies on a robust intellectual community.] Whereas the written dissertation weaves together evidence and theory within (hopefully) well constructed prose in order to convince readers of a particular interpretation, digital work weaves together evidence, theory, software libraries, and even display frameworks in creating the final result, where the software and display frameworks contribute intellectual work of their own in directly shaping the final product.

As a result in part of these concerns, the Department of History and Art History at George Mason University has included the requirement for a self-reflective process statement to be included as part of digital dissertation projects submitted to the department. This statement, which follows, is required to give a "full accounting of the technical and analogue work that went into building the digital dissertation" as well as the "code and software employed to produce the final dissertation." While traditional dissertations may also benefit from such statements, particularly as software increasingly makes complex computational processes easier for non-technical users and such work is incorporated into standard research, the process of documenting the technical structure of the project aids in its evaluation as a complex and multi-layered work of scholarship. Much of the information found here is also documented throughout the dissertation itself, as part of my argument is that the technical elements are as much part of the intellectual work of the dissertation as the more traditional narrative prose. However, in providing an overview of the technical whole of the project in this space, readers can quickly orient themselves to the structure of the project. Additional, this essay serves as a guide for reproducing the research and presentation elements of the final dissertation project.

The process statement is divided into three sections. The first gives the rationale for why I undertook this project as a digital dissertation, and summarizes the argument that I am looking to make through both the structure and the content of the full project. The second section provides a summary and accounting of the three primary layers of the dissertation: the data, the analysis, and the presentation interfaces. This section documents the software used for developing each layer and provides the information necessary for reconstituting those elements, should the project either cease to live online or should the reader wish to rerun elements for evaluation or for extension of the project. Finally, the third section of the essay takes a step back and gives a brief reflection on the project as a "digital dissertation," reflecting on the status and future of such projects within the current academic landscape.

Project Rationale

My goal in constructing A Gospel of Health and Salvation as a "digital dissertation" has been to use the medium of a browser-based interface to bring together historical and computational analysis in a way that highlights the intellectual work involved in both. In my analysis, I have focused on the affordances of the presentation medium and the epistemological requirements of the different forms of analysis I have employed.

Along the way, in seeking to solve different problems for the dissertation, I have learned enough programming to be dangerous (and to qualify for jobs as a junior web developer). I have also learned from the standards of practice in programming, data science, and library science for managing these new forms of content - datasets, code, and visualizations - in ways that are documented, readable, and reusable. Most significantly, however, is that process of learning to use different methods of computational analysis has made me increasingly aware of the fundamental epistemological questions involved in combining the divergent methods of meaning making in data science and history. This is the question that has become the underlying focus of the dissertation - how does one construct an argument using computational analysis that is both computationally sophisticated and historically meaningful?

Digital Sources

As with any digital project, A Gospel of Health and Salvation is dependent upon digital sources upon which to operate. The digital sources were produced by the Seventh-day Adventist denomination. At the time of writing, these files were hosted at the Online Archive of the denomination's Office of Archives, Statistics, and Research. The files are also increasingly available through the Adventist Digital Library, a compilation archive for a range of historic SDA materials.

`Chapter two <>`_ of the dissertation describes the processes by which I selected, evaluated, and cleaned the textual data from the digital files. That work is also documented in the `Gather <>`_, `Clean <>`_, and `Preprocess <>`_ Notebooks. I do not offer my own interface for accessing the digital files as part of this project, recommending instead that they be accessed and viewed through the infrastructure of the SDA. I can make the processed text that was used during the topic modeling phase of the project available on request. However, that text should also be recreatable using the original scans and the associated notebook files.

Computational Analysis

From automating the download of the denominational periodicals to visualizing the topic model, this project has relied on computational methods at each stage of the project. I have undertaken most of that work in Python, as documented in the included `Notebooks <>`_ section of the dissertation, with some additional work in Bash.

I used Python to complete most of the computational work in this dissertation, a choice informed by it being the language I gravitated toward in the introduction to programming course at George Mason and the usefulness of IPython Notebooks (now Jupyter Notebooks) for creating executable documents. In the time since I started on this project, R has become the default choice for computational work in the digital humanities, with its own solutions for reproducible work. While I would now recommend others start with R because of that strong community of practice, Python has the advantage of being a more generalized language, and has been particularly useful to me for the data preparation aspects of the project. Digital Interfaces

Selecting and developing the interfaces for the dissertation proved to be an interesting and significant challenge, and one that could suffice as a stand alone dissertation. While a single integrated application is the goal for a future iteration of the project, the constraints of the dissertation have resulted in this more modular approach to the different, integrated, components of the projects. There are four main interfaces for interacting with the dissertation. The first is the main project website, located at dissertation.jeriwieringa.com. The second are the GitHub repositories for the main site and the code notebooks. The third is the topic model browser. And the forth is the code library and documentation, hosted in both GitHub and ReadtheDocs.

I created the main dissertation website using Nikola, a Python static site generator. This site generate enabled me to include a variety of different formats as part of the single project, including the notebook files, html, markdown, and reStructuredText. The default styling for the site uses the Bootstrap framework, which I have modified to some extent for my design purposes. The notebook pages are included within the body of the dissertation in a static format - they can be viewed as last run but not executed. As described above, these files rely on a Jupyter server to execute, which can either be run on a server or an individual laptop.

The main topic model browser is a application of Andrew Goldstone's Topic Model Browser, which relies on D3.js. Using the output from MALLET and the scripts provided as part of the browser, I transformed the data so that it could be interacted with in this format. One of the challenges of working with topic models is that the expansiveness of the data generated makes it difficult to understand through reading. Instead, visualization provide a useful abstraction for seeing how the different topics are composed, how different documents are represented, and how these shift over time. The labels for the topics are manually generated, as described in Chapter 3. [Hopefully, I will have time to make some modifications to the browser to enable additional displays, such as topic highlighted words from the original textual data and additional filtering by metadata variables.]

Finally, I captured a number of the more repeatedly used functions into a Python library for ease and stability of use. This library is necessary for testing the code included in the project notebooks and offers examples for adoption and extension in other contexts. To aid that process, I included documentation within the Python code and generated stand-alone documentation using the Sphinx Python library. This documentation is currently hosted on ReadtheDocs.org.

Archiving and Reconstructing A Gospel of Health and Salvation

Submitting a project such as this as a dissertation raises a whole myriad of questions regarding the usefulness of existing processes and platforms for complex digital objects. The current system for archiving dissertations at George Mason relies on the use of a DSpace repository, which is optimized for the collection and preservation of singular, preferably PDF, files. Additionally, the formatting requirements for dissertations assume a textual final product, one that can be created with Microsoft Word. For the submission and archiving of this dissertation, I chose to pursue a hybrid strategy. This essay, along with the dissertation overview and bibliography, compose the "dissertation object" for this project - the primary object that is properly formatted, cataloged, and archived in the repository.

For archiving and preserving the digital project, I pursued a two tiered strategy. First, to capture the appearance of the project at the point of submission, each web interface as been captured using WebRecorder.io. This includes dissertation.jeriwieringa.com, browser.dissertation.jeriwieringa.com, and goh.readthedocs.io. These interfaces are preserved within .warc (WebARChive) files and are viewable through a web archive player. For capturing the code and data of the project, I archived the code and data files for the different dissertation components and documented the required software and versions for running the code. These files can be downloaded and run on a local machine to test different aspects of the project or to modify them for other uses. This approach captures both the reading experience of the digital dissertation, as well as its technical underpinnings. These collections are preserved in the George Mason Institutional Repository and linked to from the dissertation PDF.

Recreating the Interfaces

While the goal is for the project to continue to live on the servers of GitHub.com and ReadTheDocs.org for the foreseeable future, it is likely that these services will eventually either become incompatible with the project or cease to offer free hosting. In that event, the project can be recreated from the code and data files archived with the university library. Each code grouping has an abbreviated README file that describes the software dependencies of the project and offers installation instructions for running in a new environment. It may be necessary to recreate the operating system-level environment in order to run the relevant software. As it is hard to anticipate what the future of computing software will look like, that process is left up to the reader to determine at this time.^[A more integrated solution would be to bundle the whole of the project inside a containerized package, which includes both the operating system elements and the program code.] With the local environment and the necessary software, the readers should be able to recreate, and in the case of the notebooks, re-execute the different elements of the dissertation project.

Technical Support

When I started working on the main part of the dissertation, my programming experience to date had been the two required digital history courses at George Mason University, an additional introduction to programming course, some hands-on experience in web development through my research assistantship at the Roy Rosenzweig Center for History and New Media, and a Rails Girls workshop. In retrospect, embarking on a technical project from that starting point was a bit over-ambitious. My initial design of the project included network analysis from the denominational Yearbooks and geospatial analysis of people, publications, and ideas, along with text analysis. While I still think that these additional modes of analysis would help illuminate the development of this particularly group of people, these ideas have been bracketed for future iterations of the project.

I have been overly determined in this project to do my own computational work, both because I am committed to the idea that one needs to grapple with the assumptions and implementation of both computational and historical analysis when bringing the two modes of inquiry together and because of the gender politics of the field. I am, however, deeply indebted to many people who have given their time and energy to help me understand and troubleshoot the technical aspects of this project. Chief among these are Fred Gibbs, the Experimental Humanities Group at the Iliff School of Theology, Lincoln Mullen, who consulted on the unfortunately tabled network analysis piece of the project, Amanda Regan, Taylor Arnold, and Lauren Tilton. The computational work in this project is primarily my own, aside from a myriad of snippets gleaned from StackOverflow and the libraries and resources noted above. The one external contribution is the workflow for moving model files from DigitalOcean, where I ran Mallet due to the size of the corpus, to AmazonS3 for storage and locally for use, which was set up by Jason Wieringa.

Digital Dissertations in History: Reflections

Despite the Internet and the World Wide Web being part of the modern information infrastructure for nearly thirty years, dissertations in history that leverage the digital interfaces are still rare. In her digital dissertation, the first such project for the department of History and Art History at George Mason in 2016(?), Dr. Celeste Sharpe notes that digital dissertations fall into a gray area in the current publishing and analysis landscape.^[@Sharpe2016] While my dissertation sidesteps the issues of copyright by working with pre-1923 (and therefore public domain) documents, the format of the digital dissertation leaves the work outside of the normal structures of submission, archiving, and publication. This is true for the full range of potential digital dissertations in history, from digital public history websites to interactive games and software.

For history, the dissertation has in recent years come to serve as the prototype or first draft of the scholar's first monograph. As such, the required format and pacing of the dissertation has shifted to ease that transition from dissertation to book, reducing the requirements for historiographical surveys and emphasizing tight analysis and timely topics. For a digital dissertation, by contrast, the trajectory to the first book is less clear. While scholarly presses are increasingly interested in digital monographs and investing in platforms for their publication, these platforms on the whole envision a narrative text supplemented by multimedia elements, such as annotated images, video files, or interactive visualizations.^[Examples include Manifold Press, Fulcrum, Stanford, ...] While such a vision captures much for the work being done, it also constrains the possibilities for innovative work, as survival in the academic environment depends in no small part on ones ability to secure publication for ones work.

Additionally, bringing computational analysis into the conversation, as I do in A Gospel of Health and Salvation, raises important questions about what is required of scholarship that relies on computation and statistical modeling. This dissertation could be submitted as a textual object with supporting interactive visualizations. However, part of the argument of the dissertation, and one that is currently being made quite effectively in the sciences, is that the computational work for the dissertation is also part of the final dissertation object - the analysis of the dissertation cannot be fully evaluated without access to the computational work in a format where that work can be executed.^[A parallel argument can be made, I think, for digital public history projects, where the full web-based experience constitutes the scholarly object. This also is a format of scholarship that does not fit well into a text-first publishing framework but instead exist independently, raising questions of how to determine "publication" for academic settings.] ...

That said, the experience of attempting to re-examine the format and methods of historical analysis in history through a historical study of Seventh-day Adventism, while productive, has been a challenge. The range of questions and problems involved in creating a digital dissertation such as this pushes the boundaries of what can, or should, be tackled within the scope of a dissertation, a credentialing work within academics. This is due in large part because working in digital mediums and using computational algorithms blurs the boundaries between existing disciplinary areas. As a result, the criteria for what constitutes a scholarly production are unclear, as is the academic home for such such research. Such projects need to exist, but would benefit from being collaborative endeavors, resulting in multiple dissertations or scholarly works each of which contributes to the scholarly conversation, be that in terms of methods or in terms of particular content.

While reconsidering the format for dissertations is a useful first step in encouraging scholarship to embrace the opportunities made available with the rise of computational systems, the full embrace of these technologies is limited by the model of single-author scholarship still dominant in the humanities. A project such as mine could be the site of multiple dissertation-level projects in digital humanities, focused on different aspects from data collection and the uses of computational algorithms in analysis to interface design and interweaving the various modes of analysis. To be successful, such a project would not separate the "content expert" from the technical work, as the two are fundamentally intertwined. Such projects should be founded in the research questions of the humanities field, with all aspects of the project working to engage that larger motivating question in different formats, from how the data represents the subject matter, how the algorithms model the subject matter, how the interface illuminates or obscures the argument. Such work requires more of a lab model for dissertation research, where all involved parties share the overall agenda of the lab and the elements of the project form the basis for multiple dissertation projects. Such a model would improve the overall quality of the final projects, would increase the opportunities for mentorship, and would provide training in the collaborative work that takes place for professional academics, public scholarship, and "alt ac" jobs.

Additionally, a full embrace of "digital humanities" research in history would benefit from supporting a wider range of research agendas than is currently the case. While useful, the requirements for digital dissertation at George Mason require the construction of a "historical" argument, an intervention within the existing interpretive literature of the field. It is worth considering if this requirement unduly constrains the types of scholarship that can, and needs to be, productively undertaken within the discipline, especially with consideration of methods. There is a range of research needed investigating the methodological and epistemological consequences of adopting the methods of data science as part of historical scholarship. To also require such work make a substantive argument regarding a particular historical debate is to overextend, and thereby limit, the work that can be done by any individual researcher. One reason for this dual approach is the realities of the academic job market, where content areas job clearly dominate particularly in history. Looking ahead, however, there is potential for "digital history" jobs become more prevalent, especially with growing institutional support for data science initiatives. In that scenario, scholars focused on evaluating and developing methods for computational historical research and on developing visualization strategies and libraries for work with the questions and data of history are deeply important, particularly for the ongoing health of historical research. In expanding the model of historical scholarship to include multi-authored and methodology focused work, [something profound]

These shifts would require even more institutional and cultural change than allowing multimedia dissertations, a shift that is itself challenging given requirements around publishing for tenure and promotion. There are real questions, however, at the intersection of the humanities and technology and it behooves academic departments to innovate in terms of the model of scholarship in order to engage those questions. From exploring the rise of our current data-drive culture to evaluating the epistemological and ethical consequences of the growing reliance on data science methodologies in policy creation, social organization, and decision making, scholars trained at this intersection of historical inquiry and data science methodologies are uniquely able to contribute to current discussions and to prepare students for a wide range of roles in the current information economy. This work is something of a bridge piece, attempting to both engage directly with the epistemological shifts embedded within computational technologies and methods, as well as make an intervention into the existing literature in religious history. My hope, however, is that the field continues to expand in the future, able to support historical research focused primarily on the questions of methods and methodology made pressing in the digital age.
