The Emergence of Seventh-day Adventism

Jeri E. Wieringa

2018-02-01 00:00

The early years of the nineteenth century were a period of religious innovation and revival in the United States. From the rapid growth of previously minority denominations such as Methodists and Baptists to the creation of new religious movements such as Barton Stone’s Christians and Joseph Smith’s Latter Day Saints. While the revivals of the period are generally described as marked by enthusiasm and emotional displays, the period was also marked by a “democratization” of access to religious truths, with careful study of the Bible held up as the primary source of authority, rather than religious training or position. One example of this expansion of authority and the belief that the truths of Scripture were open to all who applied diligent study is William Miller, a one-time deist and grandson of a Baptist minister. In the 1810s, following a conversion back to the Baptist faith after his service in the War of 1812, Miller began studying his Bible in order to come to a “rational” understanding of the more prophetic passages, convinced that a rational and omnipotent God would not have given humanity a flawed guide by which to learn his will for creation. His study lead him to the conclusion that Jesus would return in the 1840s. By the late 1830s and with the help of one Joshua Himes, Miller’s interpretation was known throughout the United States, and the date solidified to between March 1843 and March 1844. Those convinced by Miller’s interpretation increasingly found themselves at odds with their home denominations, some forming new Millerite or adventist meeting groups outside of the existing church structures. When March 1844 passed within event, believers returned to the prophet texts to identify their error. One adventist preacher, Samuel Snow, proposed a new date as the correct interpretation of Scripture, October 22, 1844, which was eventually accepted by William Miller and other adventist leaders. This was the last date around which the adventist community was able to achieve consensus. When October 22 passed without event, the adventist community began to wrestle with the meaning of their Great Disappointment. One group of adventists began to follow the seventh-day sabbath teaching of Joseph Bates and the prophetic visions of the young Ellen Harmon White. It was this group of adventist believers that came to form the Seventh-day Adventist Church.

The development of Seventh-day Adventism offers a unique opportunity to examine the relationship between end-times beliefs and religious culture in American history, as seen in ideas about gender, health, and salvation. On the one hand, Seventh-day Adventism fits many of the patterns that marked religious revivalism of the early to mid nineteenth century: the embrace of a new understanding of the Bible that brought all into alignment, the embrace of charismatic religious figures in Miller and then the Whites, a willingness to suspend typical gender norms as part of the evidence of last days and the mission of the denomination, an urgent emphasis on salvation and the belief that the end times were at hand. However, such similarities also obscure some of the instructive differences between the denomination and the broader Protestant culture. While echoing the growing emphasis on women’s role in the home and related to family development, the denomination continued to honor a woman, Ellen White, as a prophet and made space for women’s labor as lecturers, missionaries, medical professionals, and teachers. Their approach to health include both an early emphasis on divine healing and a gradual embrace of medical intervention, within the framework of following God’s natural law. Their understanding of end-times and salvation positioned the government of the United States as the anticipated adversary, rather than the protagonist of God’s unfolding plan.

This chapter provides historical context for understanding the development of the Seventh-day Adventist denomination. Embedded within the revival movements of the nineteenth-century, the Adventists drew from and were a part of larger Protestant movements of the time. At the same time, their particular approaches to the social and theological problems of the day help illuminate more of the variety of American religious expression, as the religious culture they developed stands in contrast to many of the standard categories used to describe nineteenth-century religion. In this chapter,I first examines the historical roots of the movement, from the role of women in the revivals of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries and the theological trends that shaped revival religion of the period. Next, I look at the denominational roots of those who became leaders of the adventist and Seventh-day Adventist movement. Third, I provide an overview of the origins of the SDA in the teaching of William Miller, his predictions of the second coming in 1843/1844, and the development of the adventist movement. Finally, I discuss the reinterpretation of those beliefs and the fracturing of the denomination in the years after 1844. All this sets the stage for 1849 and the launching of James and Ellen White’s first publication, The Present Truth, aimed at preaching the new, and developing, seventh-day message to the scattered adventist community, a message that would form the foundation for the creation of the new Seventh-day Adventist denomination.

A note on terms: The labels used to describe different aspects and groups of believers who anticipated the second coming in 1843/1844 vary in usage and require definition. For this study, I use “adventist” to refer to those Protestant Christians who embraced William Miller’s interpretation of the Bible and expected the second coming (or second advent) during the period from 1843 to 1844. In the years following, a number of religious groups formed out of the Great Disappointment, and I use “Adventist” to refer to those formal denominations formed after the Albany conference and who concluded that they had been incorrect in their interpretation of biblical prophecy. Finally, Seventh-day Adventist or SDA refers to those believers who, after 1844, embraced the combination of adventist beliefs about end times with Seventh day Baptist teachings on the ten commandments and Saturday as the required day for Christian worship.

Women and the Revivals of the Early Republic

The early nineteenth-century was a period in American history marked by religious innovation, both in the rise of new religious traditions and in the religious understandings that people embraced. The separation of religion from state control, a process known as disestablishment, created an environment particularly ripe for a “democratization” of religious expression.2 People were increasingly able to worship according to their preference and to establish churches outside of the control of the Anglican (Episcopal) church in the South and the Congregational church in New England. As a result of disestablishment, the Baptist and Methodist traditions, which had existed both in the colonies and in Britain and Europe prior to the Revolution, flourished in the American context. Membership in Methodist churches grew to 250,000 by 1820 and 500,000 by 1830, nearly four percent of the population, and Baptists saw a similar increase, with membership growing “tenfold” in the decades after the Revolution.3 The loosening of social controls on religious expression also resulted in increasing diversity among religious traditions, as new churches, denominations, and movements formed in response to differences in religious thought and practice. Out of this period of religious innovation came some of the most distinctive American religious and social movements, including the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter Day Saints, the Oneida Utopian Community, and the Seventh-day Adventists.4

The decentralization of control, however, is not sufficient to explain the rapid proliferation of new religious movements, each claiming to present the truth that others were failing uphold or communicate. Looking more closely at religious revival and reform movements, it becomes clear that these religious actors were guided by particular visions of sacred history, by particular understandings of the arc of history and the role of American Protestant Christians in that narrative. While each group understood itself as standing at the crux of the story of salvation, all shared a framework that “began in Eden, developed in the world, and would culminate in a world without end.” The narrative was radically expansive, encompassing “humankind – saints and sinners, men and women, common and chosen…” along with “the suprahuman character of God Himself, His son the Christ, and the sworn enemy – the Antichrist.”5 In the march toward the last judgment, each group identified itself as the chosen one whose members successfully understood and obeyed the requirements for salvation, whether those requirements were correct behavior, correct attitude toward the divine, or the proper experience to ensure a connection with and the favor of the Divine. For many, and particularly for those traditions coming out of New England Puritanism, that position also required bringing about change in the world, either by converting souls through revival or by advocating for virtuous living. These various Protestant groups saw themselves as having a divine mission to prepare the world for the coming end and to do their part to ensure that events unfolded according to the Divine plan.

Protestant women as well as men embraced these various efforts to redeem the world ahead of the imminent return of Christ. As has been noted by Ann Braude and others, religion in the American context has always been a site of significant female activity.6 Although leadership positions have been restricted to male adherents across most of the American religious traditions, women have consistently constituted the majority of those who attend, who contribute both time and money to religious initiatives, and who work to train children within the religious tradition.7 Building on the existing predominance of religious women, the Protestant reform movements of the early nineteenth-century called upon women to help bring about revival and reform. As Carroll Smith-Rosenberg has noted, although these movements were generally led by “male clerics and reformers … women were their most zealous adherents.”8 In addition to joining radical religious movements, women worked for reform in a variety of ways, from fundraising for various causes to creating female benevolent societies to themselves taking to the pulpit and preaching to convince others to repent for the kingdom of heaven was at hand. Through all these efforts, women created “a public and powerful role for themselves as a female conscience and moral voice crying in a wilderness of male corruption” during the same period that domesticity and “the Cult of True Womanhood” grew in cultural prominence.9

Radical religious movements attracted the involvement of women and presented new opportunities for them to claim leadership roles that had been previously been denied. Sociologists and historians have linked periods of revival and radical religion to periods of social and economic change and distress, when “boundaries are no longer clearly defined.”10 Revivals, as “liminal” periods, provided space for the reconfiguration of social norms and structures, enabling women and others traditionally excluded from leadership roles to experiment with different constructions and in so doing, shift the shape of the social spheres.11 For the revivals of the Second Great Awakening, the social reorganization launched with the American Revolution and the instability of society brought about with increased mobility and the loosening of government control on the expression of religion created the environment in which groups traditional excluded - those without formal training in divinity, women, and ethnic minorities - could experiment with alternative structures of religious practice and qualification.

For the women drawn to radical religion, their involvement took a variety of forms, from active preaching to more gender-conforming support roles, and was explained in a variety of ways. While preaching and teaching in front of mix gender groups was constituted in many ways the height of women’s break from the cultural norms, there were additional avenues open to those who did not directly feel the call to preach. From working within the growing outreach organizations of the revival movements as “social workers, teachers, managers, missionaries, and writers” to encouraging revivals “through home meeting, prayer groups, and voluntary and missions work,” women found a range of opportunities where they could contribute to the cause.12 The “social” space of religious life, positioned as it was between the “public” and “private,” between commerce or government and the family, provided a range of opportunities for women to apply their faith.13 In defending their assumption of more forward roles in the promotion of the faith, religious women deployed a variety of strategies to navigate the balance between their religious convictions and the religious and cultural constraints. For some, and particularly for adventists, Scripture itself could be invoked for support, particularly Joel’s prophecy that in the last days God’s spirit would be poured out on both young men and women. As a result, “the sight of a woman standing in the pulpit had cosmic significance: she was a living symbol of the last days.”14 For others, they relied on what became a standard set of strategies for situating themselves and their calling within an acceptable religious framework. Nearly all in telling their story inform their audience of their strong resistance to the call, and often of their attempts to avoid it. Eventually, however, they find that they cannot resist, and so appeal to the listeners sympathy in claiming that “the authority of God outweighs that of … parents, husbands, and friend, even that of their own conscience.”15 By separating their activity from “ambition” or personal desire, and presenting themselves as weak and (somewhat) unwilling vessels, women within revival movements were able to find an unstable ground on which to claim some authority within revival movements.

Coming as it did at the end of the Second Great Awakening period, the involvement of women in both adventism and Seventh-day Adventism took on a slightly different character from earlier American revival movements. On the whole, where earlier radical revivals were often seen as appealing to the poorer classes of society, Miller’s followers were largely more economically stable and educated. While emotional appeal was certainly still part of the movements success, the emphasis for lecturers and others was on correct interpretation of the prophetic texts. This requirement extended to the women who lectured on Miller’s interpretations and contributed to the movement’s publications.16 Additionally, the vision of female religious involvement within early adventism was one tempered by the growing cultural emphasis on female domesticity. Many women adventist preachers pursued their callings from within the context of marriage, arguing “that married women, not single women, were best suited for careers in the pulpit. By claiming to speak as their husbands’ helpmates, female preachers tried to counter accusations that they were promiscuous, shameless, or”masculine.“17 This strategy enabled women to continue to claim a role for their particular gifts, even during a period when gender boundaries were resolidifying. At the same time, however, the adventist belief in the swiftly approaching second coming also tempered the encouragement toward marriage for young adherents. Ellen White’s prophetic career launched without the benefits of marriage, and she was initially reluctant to marry James White lest it imply”a denial of faith in an imminent second coming.“18 As in this case, for many early adventists, navigating the opposing pulls of cultural norms and religious beliefs often proved challenging, and required creative reinterpretations of both.

The Making of the Adventists

William Miller and adventism arose on the American scene toward the end of the period now known as the Second Great Awakening. This period, date typical from the Cane Ridge revival of 1801 to the Great Disappointment of the Millerites in 1844, contributed to shaping of American evangelicalism, solidifying a cultural commitment to revival methods, to the Bible as the sole guide of belief, the central role of the United States in the divine plan, and to the centrality of the relationship of the individual with God.

The Religious Roots of Adventism

Building on the wider revival traditions of the time, adventism drew from and shared a number of key features that marked the religious awakenings of the time. Key among these were an emphasis on recapturing the religion of the early Church, a commitment to a literal understanding of scripture, the embrace of millennialism, and an affinity for reform movements, from temperance and health reform to abolitionism. These shared commitments helped make Miller’s teaching legible to his audiences and his promise that the second coming was soon to occur fit within the religious understanding of many. Additionally, Miller’s followers came from a number of Protestant traditions, and brought with them aspects of their early theological homes into what would develop into the Seventh-day Adventist church. In identifying these trends and sources of adventist and Seventh-day Adventist theological thought, I lay the foundation for highlighting the continuities and discontinuities between this new religious denomination and the surrounding religious culture with which it developed.

Shared across multiple of the religious movements that developed during the period of the Second Great Awakening was an underlying commitment to identifying and recovering the Christianity of the early church, an impulse known as primitivism or restorationism. This impulse was not new to the reformers of the early nineteenth-century, but the range and scope of its application is noteworthy. While the impulse to cut out elements of religious faith and practice that were seen as unscriptural additions had marked Protestant Christianity since the Reformation, by the early nineteenth-century seemingly all aspects of the faith were open to challenge and reevaluation, as religious leaders sought to recover and reinstate “the truth” of Christianity as expressed in the days of the apostles.

In exploring the particularly strong hold of this approach to the past within the nineteenth century and American protestantism more broadly, scholars have looked to identify the distinctive features of the environment that made this approach particularly potent. Nathan Hatch has attached the particularly strong manifestation of restorationism of the period to two new conditions of the time: the breakdown of older systems of authority with the democratic revolutions of the age and the experience of religious pluralism. On the one hand, the political environment with the establishment of a democratic system and the rejection of older, monarchical forms of government was seen as having implications within the sphere of religion, with the need to also overthrow older, more authoritarian forms of religious organization. On the other hand, the very abundance of forms of religious expression seemed to indicate that the truth was not yet found. As a result, religious leaders and innovators sought to bring order to the surrounding chaos, preaching that they had found, through careful study of Scripture or through direct revelation, God’s true purpose and desired form of religious observance.19

The primary guide by which these efforts to recover and restore the early church were launched was the Bible. Dating back to the eighteenth-century revivals of the Great Awakening and the earlier Reformation creed of Sola Scriptura or “Scripture Alone”, Protestant Christians had long been taught an internalized the belief that the Bible offered a clear guide to the Christian life, and even more broadly, to revealing God’s plan for the world. Rather than the creeds or tradition, the Bible was held up as the sole arbiter of true faith and the primary mechanism for salvation.20 For the Bible alone to serve as the central guide of Christian life, however, it needed to be a reliable and clear source for religious truth. Religious leaders such as Alexander Campbell preached the Bible as “a book of ‘plain facts’ to be read and apprehended by all,” with careful and engaged study the solution to all differences of interpretation.21 Originally a position embraced by clergy to counter the religious power of first the Catholic Church and later points of theological orthodoxy such as the Trinity and limited atonement (the belief that only some are saved), by the nineteenth century lay believers had come to embrace the belief that the Bible, and individual interpretation thereof, was the final authority in matters of religious truth.22

One topic where this shift to approaching the Bible as a literal guide to truth and the search for the primitive church was felt particularly strongly was in the embrace of millennialism, or the belief in the second coming and thousand year reign of Christ on earth. While a minority belief during much of church history, interest in end times and the millennium increased in the years following the American, and particularly the French Revolutions, as the power of the Pope as seen to be on the decline. New studies of prophecies fueled speculation that linked the decline of the church with the restoration of the Jewish people in Palestine and saw the judgment as focused on Christendom, particularly the period of the Roman Catholic Church.24 This interpretation of scripture was informed and shaped by the embrace among millennial believers of literalism in their approach to the Bible. Where earlier interpretations of the prophetic books of Daniel and Revelation focused on their content as symbolic or alegorical, eighteenth and nineteenth century millennialists focused on the same passages as communicating a literal second coming marked by signs both strange and miraculous.25

Nineteenth-century revivalism tended to embrace one of two different visions of the millennium. The first, associated with more reform-minded religious leaders, anticipated the thousand year reign of Christ on earth and brought about by the increasing piety and conversion of (particularly) the American people. Encouraged by the success of the American Revolution in bringing about a dramatic change in style of government, these believers anticipated a similar revolution in the social sphere, one that would bring about a culture marked by justice, equality, and virtuous behavior.26 The gradual salvation and conversion of the world was the historical arc foretold in Scripture and it was the role of believers to help bring about conversion and the gradual redemption of society. The second, embraced by Miller and others, took a more pessimistic view and posited that the second coming of Christ would precede the establishment of the thousand year period of peace (here ruled over by the returned figure of Jesus.) For Miller, this second coming would arrive swiftly and for later Seventh-day Adventists, would come at the close of a period of intense persecution of those who followed the law and worshiped on Saturday. For adventist and Seventh-day Adventist, the historical arc was one of struggle between the faithful and the world, between God and Satan, would end in the sudden return and triumph of Jesus. The role of believers in this vision is to be prepared and faithful, as well as to convert others to the faith, for the second coming cannot occur until all have the opportunity to be saved.27

End times expectation was not unique to Miller and his adventist followers. In the face of the social and political changes that shaped post-Revolutionary American society, many religious revivalists embraced some form of millennial rhetoric and expectation. Noted preacher Charles Finney went so far as to claim in his Lectures on Revival that should the church embrace revivals and bring about the necessary conversions, “the millennium may come in this country in three years.”28 Even among religious movements that positioned themselves in contrast to the general culture, such as Latter Day Saints, there still existed an underlying impulse to nationalistic millennialism, as members believed that “God’s kingdom would yet rise in America … and their endeavors would serve as decisive leaven.”29 Committed to the truth of their particular religious interpretation, believers from multiple traditions set out to make ready the way ahead of their soon to be returning savior and to prepare themselves in advance of the coming judgment.

One final cultural contributor to the development of Seventh-day Adventism to discuss is reform, from abolitionism to temperance, health reform to women’s rights. In addition to a flurry of religious innovation, the early nineteenth century also saw the rise of numerous reform efforts, groups of citizen organizing together to bring about some sort of societal change. These efforts were often framed in religious terms, as reformers saw themselves as part of the divine plan for and unfolding of the world. The beginning and end of this plan were known: creation began in the garden of Eden and would end with the establishment of God’s eternal kingdom. And human actors lived through and contributed to that progression, setting in motion the events that propelled God’s plan and using their vision of the unfolding “cosmic drama” to guide their actions and interpret the events of their lives.30

Reformers sought to bring about order and to right social wrongs within the context of a increasingly diverse and decentralized society and as such was often tied to millennial visions. From temperance reform to abolitionism, reformers were “bent upon sacralizing all the world’s order in accordance with their vision of God’s plan.”31 Once the reform goal was achieved, the next phase of God’s plan - the second coming or the millennial period - could come into being. However, as reformers often discovered, their mission to redeem the world was difficult at best. Convinced that their work was within God’s plan, reformers were left searching for reasons they were not more successful, or, when they were, for reasons that such successes did not usher in the millennium as anticipated. This tension fueled the embrace of multiple reform efforts but also pushed members to the edges of their belief in the millennial vision. Advocates for women’s rights, for example, while initially inspired by the millennial beliefs of the early nineteenth century often found themselves outside the bounds their initial religious homes, and seeking a transformation outside the bounds of Christian millennialism. Still seeking to right the social wrongs and bring about a different organization of the world, they left behind the particulars of the old religious framework, seeking new understandings of sin and the path to salvation in communitarian experiments, in transcendentalism, in spiritualism, and in other “post-Christian faiths.”32

William Miller, Joshua Himes, and the Creation of a Movement

Of the varied religious movements that developed during the nineteenth-century, one of the more notable were the Millerites, American Protestants drawn largely from Baptist and Methodist congregations who were convinced by the teaching of one William Miller that the Bible revealed that the second coming, or second advent, would take place around the year 1843. Miller, a commissioned militia officer during the War of 1812 and if his biographer is to be believed, a leading citizen of Poultney, Vermont, began his study of the Bible shortly after his conversion from Deism in an attempt to prove the reliability of the Bible by reconciling all apparent inconsistencies he discovered.36 In 1818, he first came to the conclusion that the prophecies, particularly Daniel, revealed that the second coming would take place around year 1843. After spending five years confirming his interpretations and calculation, he began to share his conclusions, starting with family and friends, then reaching out to local ministers. Miller understood his findings as reinforcing the teachings of the churches and as part of the prevailing belief in the second coming and the need for salvation. He slowly began to receive invitations to preach in a variety of churches, including Baptist, Methodist, and Congregational.37 In 1832, he published his findings in the Vermont Telegraph and in 1833, he was granted a preaching license from his Baptist church in Hampton, New York.38 He began preaching and lecturing in churches throughout New England throughout the 1830s and into the 1840s. He published a 64 page pamphlet of his interpretation in 1834 and a series of sixteen lectures in 1836. Between 1834 and 1839, when he began to with Joshua Himes, Miller recorded that he had given about 800 lectures on his interpretation of prophecy and his belief that the time of the second coming was at hand.39

Despite publishing, traveling, and preaching widely, Miller’s teachings would have remained one of many little known local religious movements of the nineteenth century if it were not for Joshua V. Himes. Prior to meeting Miller, Himes had a long history of embracing reform movements as the way to bring about the kingdom of God. Licensed to preach by the Christian Church, he was pastor of a church in Boston, was a Temperance lecturer, started a school for boys, was on the board of the Massachusetts Anti-Slavery Society, was an early supporter of William Lloyd Garrison, and distributed periodicals and other printed materials of the Disciples of Christ.40 Anticipating the coming millennium and already working hard to bring it about, Himes found Miller’s teaching that it would arrive in a mere four years time both striking and deeply motivating. With Miller’s blessing, Himes became his chief publicist, launching a campaign of print and conferences aimed at disseminating Miller’s work as quickly and widely as possible.41 Publishing periodicals such as Signs of the Times, The Midnight Cry, the Philadelphia Alarm, and The Western Midnight Cry, among others, as newspapers of the movement, along with Miller’s The Second Advent and memoirs, as well as tracts, hymns, and prophetic charts, Himes flooded religious readers with material expounding Miller’s teachings. In doing so, he fostered a sense of religious community among adventist believers and created the mechanisms by which Miller’s message could spread.42

At the risk of projecting a modern concept onto the past, it is clear that Himes had an excellent sense of “marketing” - he knew how to engage people in a cause and how to gain, if not converts, at least attention. He worked to ensure the widest possible reach for Miller’s teachings, establishing periodicals in the key cities of Boston, New York, Philadelphia, and Cincinnati. He also understood the value of “showmanship,” acting quickly on a decision to build a “great tent,” one that seated 4,000 people and was one of the largest to be seen in America; publishing and selling colorful charts to illustrate the intricate (and obtuse) details of Miller’s interpretation; and convincing Miller to offer a more definitive date. He also began holding regular conference meetings for those convinced by Miller’s teachings. These gatherings provided a forum for the dispersed community to come together, to work to reach consensus on some of the contested details of Miller’s teaching, and to agree on a strategy for spreading the word.43

The efforts of Himes launched Miller and his teachings into the public consciousness and created a community around the belief that the second coming would occur between March 21, 1843, and March 21, 1844. In 1842, just two years after he joined the cause, Himes claimed fifty-thousand readers for Signs of the Times and between three and four hundred ministers distributing the materials.44 By 1844, Himes had overseen the foundation of a number of serial publications, tracts, and pamphlets and had organized, attended, and spoken at conferences around the United States and Canada. This scale of publication and organization is notable. While most evangelical movements of the time utilized the press to spread their message and unite believers, under the leadership of Himes, the Millerites “published tracts, memoirs, and newspapers on a scale never before imagined.”45 As a result of the successful publishing campaigns, Miller became highly sought-after speaker, claiming to have given about 4000 lectures in 500 hundred towns between 1840 and 1844. In addition to Miller and Himes, by Miller’s calculations some 200 ministers in the United States and Canada had embraced his interpretation, and an additional 500 public lecturers circulated his view.46

Miller did not set out to form a new movement or denomination. Rather he saw his teachings as within the bounds of the prevailing orthodoxy, as the logical conclusion of prevailing Protestant beliefs. He made no effort to start his own church and did not wish to be identified as a “sectarian leader,” instead encouraging followers to stay within their home denominations. In claiming success, he notes that, as result of his early lecturing, “many churches thereby greatly added to their numbers,” a statement that reinforces that he saw his goal as the revival of the local church, not the creation of his own.47 He saw his teaching of the imminent second coming to be a truth of general interest for all denominations, and one that would (or should) be embraced regardless of denominational affiliation.48

This did not always go according to plan, as the experience of the Harmon family illustrates. In her autobiography, Ellen (Harmon) White reports, “At the age of eleven I was converted, and when twelve years old was baptized, and joined the Methodist church.” Upon hearing William Miller lecture on the topic of the Second Advent at age of 13 (1841), she “then felt that I was not holy, not ready to see Jesus” and so “when the invitation was given for church members and sinners to come forward for prayer,” she went forward in the belief that “I must have a great work done for me to fit me for Heaven.”49 Although already converted, baptized, and a member of the church, the news that the second coming would occur in two years time caused White to call her faith into question. She began regularly attending “Second Advent meetings,” seeking “full and free salvation” from God.50 She received the confirmation she sought when she obeyed God’s prompting to “pray in a public prayer-meeting.”51

Convinced of her salvation and full of anticipation for the return of Jesus, she returned to her Methodist class-meeting some six months later to share “what Jesus had done for me, and what a fullness I enjoyed through believing that the Lord was coming.” This message, however, was not acceptable to the local Methodist class-leader, whom she reports interrupted her testimony to add “Through Methodism,” an addition she rejected because “it was Christ and the hope of his soon coming that had made me free.”52 Eventually the tensions between Harmon family and the local Methodist minister over their Millerite convictions became too great and the family was “at one time cast out of the Methodist Church.”53

Local communities of adventist believers emphasized regular attendance at the local Protestant Sunday services, but also attended their own interdenominational meetings of those who believed the second coming was at hand. Dedicated to sharing the news of the second coming with their neighbors and religious fellows, members of Millerite associations often found themselves at odds with their local religious leaders.54 Finding community among other adventists and meeting resistance in their local churches was often too much for individual believers, prompting them to leave their home denominations and claim an adventist identify. While Miller himself was quite distressed by this trend, it set the stage for new denominations to form in the wake of the disappointments of 1844.55

Because of the non-denominational aspects of Miller’s preaching and because multiple dates were eventually given for the second Advent, the total number of people who joined Miller in anticipating the second coming and the end of the world is difficult to determine. Miller estimated that there were 200 ministers and 500 lecturers who were had embraced his teachings. In the years after the 1844, the New York Tribune estimated that there were 30-40,000 Millerites at the movements peak. The high end of estimates puts the peak number at 100,000, though Miller himself claimed that about 50,000 embraced his message, calculating based on the number of conversions he witnessed in his lectures.56 These were the believers — those who embraced Miller’s interpretation as, quite literally, gospel truth. Given the massive amount of literature produced by Himes, however, it is likely that many more quietly marked the passage of 1844 with interested skepticism. What is clear is that, although Miller was able to preach to nearly 500,000 people, what propelled his teaching from local anticipation to an international movement was the coordinated publishing efforts of Himes.57

As would be the case for the early Seventh-day Adventists, Himes recognized the power of print and effectively deployed it as the center around which the Millerite movement formed. Regular publications spread the content of Miller’s interpretation, raising awareness of his findings. Those same publications provided the forum by which Himes organized the regular conferences, inviting interested people to gather together to coordinate their efforts to spread the news of the coming millennium, and advertised upcoming tent meetings. Reports about the successes of the camp meetings helped the community to take part in the events regardless of geographic proximity, and served as further proof of the truth and successfulness of the adventist message, for if God were not behind the movement, the events would not be successful. In addition, Himes strategically started multiple publications in different cultural and geographic centers on the U.S., increasing the coverage that the movement could achieve and catering to regional interests. This was a strategy that the Seventh-day Adventists would later emulate, first as they came together around the publication of a new interpretation of Miller’s teachings and the events of 1844, and later as they sent out missionaries, establishing printing houses in each new geographic region they entered.

Becoming Seventh-day Adventists

[ intro/abstract ]

The Fracturing of the Adventists

“The Albany Conference represented the beginning of the Adventist Christian Church, a new denomination with its own distinct beliefs and rituals. … Replacing the flexibility of their earlier churches with greater structure and discipline, they discouraged visionary enthusiasm, established a professional clergy, and forbade women to serve as evangelists.”58

As the period Miller identified between March 21, 1843 and March 21, 1844, passed, adventist believers began to develop a number of alternative theories to address the persistence of time. A temporary reprieve from the crisis caused by persistence of time was granted when a young man named Samuel Snow announced that he had identified the error in Miller’s calculations and that October 22, 1844, was the correct date indicated for the second coming. While acceptance of that interpretation brought unity back to the movement for a time, with the passing of the October 22, which came to be known as the Great Disappointment, the tensions between the various groups of adventists became too great to maintain a unified movement.

The adventist community did not collapse immediately after the final disappointment of October 22, 1844. While Himes briefly suspended publication after the October 16 issue of The Advent Herald and Signs of the Times Reporter, in anticipation of the second coming, when the day passed without event, he quickly regathered, releasing a new issue on October 30, 1844. Still convinced of the truth of the core of the adventist message, that the time of the second coming was at hand, he offered a retelling of the history of the adventist movement that emphasized the nuances of their teachings around the date of the second coming and downplayed the significance of the October date.59 However, understanding and explaining the persistence of time posed a significant challenge for the community.

Many of those who had joined the adventist cause leading up to the October date returned to their original religious homes, joined other movements, such as the Shakers, or left the faith all together. Among those who remained committed to Miller’s teachings, the movement split into two main groups: those who held that the end was indeed near but they had been mistaken about the date; and those who held that the end was indeed near but that they had been mistaken about the significance of the date. The debates between these groups took place on the pages of the main adventist papers, as well as in the pages of new periodicals started to promote various interpretations of the nature and timing of the Second Advent.60 While Miller himself continued to believe that his interpretation was correct and the second coming was at hand, he ceased to offer or support any definitive date predictions after 1844.61

Those who would come to form Seventh-day Adventism were part of the second category of movements to come out of adventism, believing that Miller had been correct in his interpretation of the Bible, and his endorsement of October 22, 1844, as the date prophesied but incorrect about what that date signified.62 They embraced what were considered some of the more radical theological positions discussed in the wake of the 1844. First, they embraced the teaching of “conditional immortality,” viewing the standard teaching of an immortal soul to be extra-biblical, arguing instead that people would be granted immortality (or not) at the resurrection. Second, this group of Adventists embraced the Seventh-day Baptist position on keeping Saturday, rather than Sunday, as the Sabbath. Third, rather than question the date Miller and Snow had given for the second coming, the founders of Seventh-day Adventism questioned the event that the date marked, holding what became known as the Sanctuary Doctrine. This was the belief that October 22, 1844, marked the beginning of Christ’s work cleansing the sanctuary, which was “heavenly,” rather than being the earth as Miller had assumed, and there Jesus was undertaking the work of “blotting out sins” ahead of his second coming. Fourth, they embraced the existence of prophetic gifts, the belief that God would speak directly through an individual to provide guidance and new revelation for a community. For the early Seventh-day Adventists, this gift was primarily limited to Ellen (Harmon) White, though early on a number of people claimed to have received visions. Finally, although this was eventually abandoned, they embraced the “shut door” doctrine, holding that October 22, 1844, marked a closing of the period of salvation and only those who had accepted adventism prior to the date could be saved.63 As with others who continued in the Adventist faith after 1844, all of these beliefs hinged on a core belief that the waiting time they were now in would be short and that the second coming would take place soon.

Ellen Harmon, James White, and the Gathering of Seventh-day Believers

As for many of their adventist contemporaries, publishing was central to the efforts of the nascent Seventh-day Adventist community to articulate and share their views. While there were a number of figures who contributed to developing what would become the main points of Seventh-day Adventist theology, the key figures for understanding the development of the denomination and the use of print to unite and grow a community of believers are James and Ellen (Harmon) White. Ellen Harmon White established herself early on as an authoritative prophetic voice for an adventist community struggling to understand how to reconcile their interpretation of the Bible and the events they experienced. James White, who had worked as a Millerite lecturer and correspondent, was one of her earliest converts and became her primary publicist. It is through their combined efforts that the Seventh-day Adventist church coalesced and grew in the years after 1844.

Starting in December of 1844, the sixteen-year-old Ellen Harmon began to experience visions related to the events of October 22, 1844, visions that provided evidence for what was considered to be the more radical interpretations of the Adventist movement and the continuation of time. Her first vision, experienced during a prayer gathering with four other young Adventist women, affirmed the central become a core principle of Seventh-day Adventism: that the October 22, 1844, date had been correctly determined and marked the end of the period of 2300 days (interpreted as years) described in Daniel 8:14. While Miller and Himes had understood the end of that “2300 day” period to correspond with the second coming, Harmon and others pointed instead to the end of the period corresponding to the start of Jesus’ judgment of souls, which they now understood as taking place in heaven, not as part of the second coming.64 The answer of what to do with the vision came one week later, as Ellen reported a second vision instructing her to “go and relate to others what he had revealed to me,” a task she was warned would come with no small about of trouble.65 Harmon embarked on the life of the religious itinerant, traveling around New England with family and friends, and sharing her vision to the local Adventist community.66

In 1845, while visiting various adventist meetings to share her visions with the community, Ellen Harmon met and gained a convert and ally in James S. White, a Christian Connection preacher and adventist lecturer. United in the mission to share the adventist message as understood through Ellen’s visions, the two began traveling together around New England, an arrangement that caused no small amount of speculation. While initially holding that marriage suggested a lack of faith in the nearness of the second coming, propriety eventually won out and on August 30, 1846 the two married.67

Both James and Ellen (Harmon) White were familiar with the adventist strategies for evangelism, which combined print, itinerant lecturing and testimony sharing, and periodic tent meetings and local conferences. James White had been an active Millerite lecturer during the years leading up to 1844 and continued as an active correspondent with adventist periodicals in the years after the Great Disappointment, including the Advent Herald, the Advent Herald and Bible Advocate, and The Second Advent Watchman, papers that represented three of the major groups that developed out of adventism.68 In these he reported back to the community about camp-meetings held and argued for theological positions, such as conditional immortality. Ellen had also been active in the local Millerite community, speaking at local churches and class meetings about her experience and her belief in the coming end of time prior to the events of 1844.69

In addition to giving testimony in person, Harmon wrote of her visions to a number of adventist leaders and publishers. In December of 1845, one year after her first post-Disappointment visions, Ellen Harmon wrote a letter to Enoch Jacobs, editor and publisher of The Day Star, originally the adventist publication, Western Midnight Herald, describing her vision of “the Advent people” on their journey along the narrow way toward heaven and affirming that those who had embraced Miller’s teachings were indeed on the “narrow path” to heaven.70 As noted in the issue of The Day Star, and a subsequent letter by Harmon, this letter was purportedly not intended for publication, however, White quickly followed up with a second publication to further clarify her message and affirm that the church had now entered a waiting period before Christ’s second coming. With her visions now circulating among the adventist communities through The Day Star and other periodicals, the couple also began to produce their own materials to share Ellen’s particular vision. In 1847, noting that the publication which they had intended to publish with was no longer producing new issues, James began to produce pamphlets and broadsheets offering his exegesis and Ellen’s visions to help the adventist community make sense of their current position in the unfolding of the end times.71 In 1846, Ellen met Joseph Bates, a leader among the Millerites and a proponent of “Seventh-day” sabbatarianism, or the belief that Saturday, rather than Sunday, was the commanded day of Christian observance. While initially skeptical of Ellen’s visions, Bates was eventually convinced, and published an account of them in 1847 with his own introduction of her as a prophet to the adventist community.72 The evangelistic culture of the Millerites and adventists provided James and Ellen a template for how to reach the community with their particular message, leverage existing networks of print and public speaking to reach a wider audience with their good news.73

While the publishing efforts of the White’s initially took the form of more singular publications, or relied on existing adventist outlets, in 1849 James White began to produce his own title, The Present Truth. His goal for the title, as described in its initial issue, was to reach the “scattered flock” with the “present truth” as quickly as possible, for the second coming was at hand.74 Central to that “present truth” was the necessity of keeping the Decalogue (the ten commandments) and the teaching that the commandment to “honor the Sabbath day” required Christian observance on Saturday (Friday sundown to Saturday sundown) rather than Sunday.75 In 1850, and at least partially in response to some criticism of the White’s efforts to create a new publication, Ellen published an account of her vision on September 23 of that year where she was shown that they were in the “gathering time” where God was bringing his people together and to a shared understanding of the adventist message, an effort that required “that the truth be published in a paper, as preached.”76

James and Ellen saw these publications initially as a short term endeavor — anticipating the quick success of their mission to bring together the adventist community around the belief that October 22 had marked the beginning of an interim period of Christ’s “investigative judgment.”77 But as time continued and interest grew in the publication, it became clear that this would be a longer endeavor. While the initial issues were overall uni-directional, offering arguments for the Sabbath and accounts of Ellen’s visions, by the third and fourth issue of The Present Truth, the publication began to take on a community building function. In Volume 1.3, published August 1849, James printed a notice about upcoming conference meetings to be held in Vermont and Maine by Joseph Bates. In Volume 1.4, published September 1849, he included a letter from “J. C. Bowles” who wrote from Jackson, Michigan to send word that they had received The Present Truth, to report on the success of Bates’ trip west, and to suggest that James “insert extracts of the letters you may receive from the brethren” as to hear such would be “comforting … to hear from others” and “may induce some to examine the subject, that would not otherwise.”78 While James notes that he did not intend initially to publish letters from the “brethren,” such quickly become a regular feature of The Present Truth and its successor, The Second Advent Review and Sabbath Herald (generally referred to as The Review and Herald). By the last issue of The Present Truth – Volume 1.11, published November 1850 – over half of the publication is devoted to letters from readers and fellow seventh-day proponents, responses to controversies within the community (in this case regarding the eating of pork), news about recent and upcoming meetings, and the sale of other literature, including hymnals and prophetic charts.79

The publication location of the White’s initial periodicals reflect the family’s, and the religious community’s, movement from upper New England toward the West. Initially based out of Maine, where the Harmon family was located, as early as 1849 their teaching had been brought as far west as Michigan through the preaching of Joseph Bates. In 1851, in order to make it easier to travel to the far reaches of the dispersed community and to more cheaply distribute the periodical, the White’s moved their publishing operation to upstate New York, first to Saratoga Springs, and in 1852 to Rochester.80 Repeating the Millerite strategy of combining publications, traveling lecturers, and tent meetings to create and hold the community together, the editors of The Review and Herald understood the role of the paper as central to that effort, that for a people “thus scattered, and surrounded by unbelief and opposition, you certainly need the weekly visits of a paper devoted to the present truth.”81 In 1855, the community in Battle Creek, Michigan, offered to establish a more permanent home for the denominations publishing efforts, including a press. By October of that year, James and Ellen relocated to Battle Creek, and the community called a general conference to decide who would run the daily operations of the publication.82 Battle Creek would remain the cultural and political center of the denomination through the rest of the century.

So central was publishing to the religious movement, that by 1860, the logistical concerns of running a successful publishing operation pushed James White and others toward discussing legal incorporation of the press and the denomination. During a general conference held in Battle Creek in the fall of 1860, called in order to discuss “the proper method of holding church property, the want of our Office of publication, &c.,” James White presented the case for incorporation.83 Noting that, as it stood, he was the sole owner of the “REVIEW office” and his desire to see the property owned instead by the church, James White led the argument for a mechanism of legal incorporation. However, this proved a culturally contentious issue for the community of adventists, many of whom understood separation from the state as central to their charge to be a separate people. While individual churches might establish a legal presence for building ownership, the press presented a different challenge for the emerging denomination — a centrally held organization that required some form of central management. Eventually a compromised was reached, creating an “association” for the purposes of legal incorporation of the publishing office and its holdings, while making membership in that association an optional aspect of church membership.84 On May 3, 1861, the Seventh-day Adventist Publishing Association was legally incorporated in Battle Creek Michigan.85 In May 1863, during the annual meeting in Battle Creek, delegates voted to create the General Conference of Seventh-day Adventists as formally organize the political structure of the denomination and outline the relationship between the smaller state conferences and the larger national organization in order to coordinate efforts for the ordination of ministers and the sharing of the Adventist message, including the distribution of the denominational literature.86

Developing a Distinctive Theology

Conclusion: Living at the End of Time

“Although White, Mussey, and Wellcome managed to preserve the Millerite legacy of female evangelism, they moved in increasingly conservative directions during the 1850s and 1860s.”87

“According to Beulah Matthewson, who published an article and a book defending female preaching, women could speak as men’s helpmates, but they certainly could not be ordained. Even though they could pray aloud, sing, witness, exhort, or preach, they were forbidden to”assume the leadership," or rob men of their rightful authority in the home and the church. … Until history came to an end, they would have to humbly accept their subordination to men.“88

Both William Miller and Ellen White came to prominence at the end of the period of the Second Great Awakening and the movements they started exemplify both the culmination of the revivals of the earlier period and their decline.

Historian James Bratt locates the climax of anti-revivalism in 1845, right as Miller’s adventists were coming to terms with the failure of their prophetic interpretation.89 Part of what he identifies as the third wave of anti-revivalism, he identifies Miller’s teaching as precipitating a re-evaluation of enthusiasm and revival methods within Methodism.90

[Bratt links EGW and Palmer together as sharing a emphasis on and search for perfection as sign of and requirement for salvation - p. 93. Where the revival-driven experiences of salvation that marked the religion of the Second Great Awakening had pointed to emotional assurance and conviction as the signs of salvation, starting in the 1840s, the religious culture shifted to a concern for perfection. For White, this perfection was tied to the following of the old testament law, particularly the ten commandments, as well as the “law of nature” in terms of health and diet. ]

“More broadly, their story points up a more complicated dialectic: that stress (in both senses of the term) on the individual gives rise to the institutional, and that in things fixed people could feel more free.”91

Although often treated in the historical literature as another example of the enthusiasm of the second great awakening, Miller and the Seventh-day Adventist church he inspired can also be understood as pushing evangelical reform to its them breaking point, articulating a vision too dissonant with the prevailing norms to be embraced. {bring in Ruth Doan}
