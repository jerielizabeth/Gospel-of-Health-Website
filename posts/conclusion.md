For all of the interest in topic modeling within the digital humanities community, the approach has been both under utilized and over praised. 

Computational methods, as should be no surprise, do not offer a way to bypass questions of interpretation or provide answers in and of themselves. Rather, they provide ways to reframe a source base or to reexamine a set of questions, and to enable pattern identification and exploration at different scales.

Historians have been skeptical about the value of text analysis for historical research, and with cause. Much of the standard tools and practices around text analysis have focused at the level of words - how words carry meaning, how those meanings shift over time. Even in cultural history, where attention to "discourse" is greater than other historical approaches, the level of analysis differs significantly.


There are an increasing range of methods available for exploring large collections of text and calculating relationship within and between the words used to communicate in a wide variety of contexts. One of the early methods to gain traction in the digital humanities, topic modeling provides a means of identifying patterns, of classifying texts, and of describing underlying or latent aspects within a corpus. Among the available algorithms, it is particularly useful in historical research as it is unsupervised (can be used on a large, undescribed, collection) and can be used with some modifications on corpora marked by messy OCR. 

However, methodologically there is little consensus around how such models can be used as part of historical research. While it is clear that a topic model can provide access to major themes within a corpus and be used to identify documents for further research, the processes for leveraging the model data are debated, and there is little consensus around even the most basic aggregation of model data, tracking over time.
