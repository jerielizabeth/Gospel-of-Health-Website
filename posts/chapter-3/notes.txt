For denominational writers and historians, the history of Ellen White is additional complicated, as her history is intertwined with that of the denomination and the development of Adventist theology.^[See @Land2014 for an historiographical overview of the scholarship on Ellen White.] Many of the denomination's distinctive theological positions are intertwined with White, substantiated by her visions and taught in her published works. Additionally, Ellen White's prophetic role in the history of the denomination has posed a challenge, as leaders have long sought to balance the advantages of claiming prophetic leadership with the challenges of that leadership coming from a female voice. From James White's early downplaying of Ellen's role to recent histories tying Ellen White's leadership to "Adventist lifestyle" rather than "doctrinal formation," the gender politics of Ellen White's foundational role in the history of the denomination continue to shape the ways she is remembered and discussed.^[During the 1850s, as part of efforts to make the denomination more respectable, James White downplayed and stopped publishing Ellen White's visions in the Review and Herald. Knight makes a point of clarifying that Ellen White's prophetic role was secondary to that of careful biblical study (carried out primarily by the male leadership of the denomination). @Knight1999, p. 37] Always present in the minds of Adventist members and critics, it is clear that Ellen White's leadership has had a significant influence on the development of the denomination, though the shape of that influence has been contested.

One of the few scholars to undertake prolonged studies of women and gender in Seventh-day Adventism has been sociologist, Laura Vance, whose work focuses on the gender dynamics of the denomination in relation to their social and cultural position. Examining the long history of the denomination, she correlates historical attitudes about gender roles with the broader relationship between the religious sect and the world, noting that roles were more expansive during the initial period of the denomination when members defined themselves in contrast to their cultural context. She notes that by the 1890s, as their "sectarian identity" begins to decline, the rhetoric within the denomination shifts toward language that echoes "the Cult of True Womanhood and the Cult of Domesticity."^[@Vance1999, p. 112] While much later than their Protestant peers, by the twentieth century authors began to emphasize the home as haven and the labor of women as particular to female labor. These shifts occurred as the SDA leadership worked to align themselves with the more established Protestant denominations and embraced a more rigid understanding of gendered spheres than had been the case during the nineteenth-century.^[@Vance1999, p.112-115] This framework, while useful for parsing the overall movement of the denomination toward more binary understandings of gender and gender roles, also introduces questions about how Ellen White's writings, and her role in the movement, fit into that general pattern. The statue of Nebuchadnezzar's dream, interpreted by Daniel as portraying to progression (and devolution) of the kingdoms to follow after Babylon, appears frequently in explorations of end-times events. The golden head of head of Babylon is followed by the silver of "Medo-Persia", and the bronze of "Greece". Next the iron legs of the Roman empire, ending in the iron and clay feet and toes of the "divided kingdom," which are parsed by later interpreters as the ten kingdoms (of ...). Each kingdom would overthrow the next until finally all would be replaced by the kingdom of heaven. 

This progression of events is held to be repeated in the four beasts of Daniel 7. Here the lion represents Babylon, followed by the bear of the Medo-Persian empire, the leopard of the Greeks, and the beast of the Romans. As with the ten toes of Nebuchadnezzar's statue, the beast of Daniel 7 features ten horns, and grows an eleventh, representing the last kingdom preceding the kingdom of heaven.
Finally, the Ram and Goat of Daniel 8 again represent the clash between the Medo-Persian empire and the Greeks. For the Seventh-day Adventist commentators, the breaking of the horn of the ram referred to the death of Alexander the great, the appearance of four horns in its place to the division of power between four military leaders, and the growth of the "little horn" with the rise of the Papacy and the "Union of Church and State."^[@Sadler1905, p. 19, 20]
