In 1855, the Seventh-day Adventist community began to wrestle directly with the question of how time ought to be organized. Having gained some amount of agreement within the community that Saturday, rather than Sunday, was the proper day for Sabbath observance, the question remained as to when the Sabbath officially began. This debate provides a microcosm of the negotiation between the mechanized time embraced more generally during the nineteenth-century and the older, "natural," time of the Bible. As noted by James White, two general positions with regard to time were debated within the early Seventh-day Adventist community. The first, which relied on the clock for time, argued that the Sabbath began at six p.m. local time. The second argued that the Sabbath began at sundown, and as such shifted in terms of clock time with the season. James notes that "We have never been fully satisfied with the testimony presented in favor of six oclock" and as such had commissioned a study of the matter from J. N. Andrews. While the business of the meetings focused largely on the status of their current efforts, including calls for an increase in the number of people engaged in spreading the Seventh-day message, included in the adopted "resolutions" was a notice that "we recognize in the present condition of the Pope of Rome and the Sultan of Turkey, unmistakable evidence that we have reached the very conclusion of the great lines of prophecy, and that our confidence in the speedy advent of our Lord is unwavering."^[@GC2007, p. 52] -->
