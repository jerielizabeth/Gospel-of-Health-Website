The problem of researchers being asked to relying on data from databases without knowledge of the underlying coverage or quality, as Spedding describes, is not unique to the ECCO project. In "Confronting the Digital" Tim Hitchcock notes that inaccurate OCR is endemic to the digital corpora that many historians, digitally focused or not, rely on for their research. For example, he notes that the [Burney Collection](http://www.gale.com/17th-and-18th-century-burney-collection/), which is a Gale product containing 17th and 18th century English newspapers, has a "word accuracy rate of 65 per cent, which drops to a rate of 48.4 per cent when looking at 'significant words' ..."^[Tim Hitchcock, "Confronting the Digital: Or How Academic History Writing Lost the Plot," Cultural and Social History 10, no. 1 (2015), 13.] This means that over half of the occurrences of those significant words in the original documents are absent from the search results, which depend on the generated text layer. The limitations of digital and digitized corpora are not readily apparent to users who relies on the provided search interface. Although digital databases and archives greatly improve access to collections, removing travel requirements and preservation restrictions, they also raise new challenges for researchers, challenges which are frequently being ignored as they are difficult to parse when the data regarding sources, processes, and quality are intentionally obscured by database providers.

One approach to this growing challenge is to reconsider how scholars understand the digital facsimiles created in the digitization process. Rather than regard digital objects as a "transparent surrogate" for the paper objects they represent, the challenge facing historians in the digital age is to understand digital artifacts and the collections in which they are located as separate entities from the originating source, to be used and cited differently from their paper counterpart.^[This is in line with Ryan Cordell's argument in "‘Q i-jtb the Raven’: Taking Dirty OCR Seriously." For Cordell, this includes taking into consideration the digitization process and the available data around selection of materials for digitization, funding sources, document sources, digitization agent(s), software, file information, and OCR accuracy. Ryan Cordell, "‘Q I-Jtb the Raven’: Taking Dirty Ocr Seriously" (2016): accessed November 16, 2016, http://ryancordell.org/research/qijtb-the-raven/.] At the very least, this can be improved by using citations to draw attention to the use of digital objects and provided search functions in online databases. By acknowledging the digital artifacts and computational processes already in use, historians would acknowledge the work of the digitizing agency as providing an important role in the selection, handling, and control of the information they use, a role that shapes what can be known from a particular dataset. While the scale of the digital resources available provides an illusion of comprehensiveness, it is vital to grapple with the ways that that the digital record inherits the limitations of the physical records from which it is derived, while adding its own.


This is not to argue that digital resources are unreliable for the historical researchers, but to make the case that the quality of the data, both in terms of scope and error-rate, are factors to be grappled with. This is true both in the case of digital history projects that use text-mining as evidence, such as this one, and traditional historical research that relies on full-text search results within the growing number of databases of primary sources. It is too easy when using digital materials to suggest a comprehensiveness and completeness that is not accurate due both to the scale of the available materials and the easy of search that digital materials make possible. As a result, it is incumbent upon the researcher using digital materials to identify the scope and quality of the underlying datasets and to factor that information into the claims they make.

Historical research of the recent past has taken place under a shared understanding of the sources, primarily the archival holdings of institutions and governments, and method of interpretation, that of reading and recognizing patterns. Ian Milligan, in his study of dissertations on Canadian history from 1997 to 2010, discovered a significant increase in the citation rates for Canadian newspapers that had been digitized and made available through online databases.^[Milligan computed a 991% increase in references to the *Toronto Star* and a 926% increase in reference to the *Globe and Mail* between 1998 and 2010, a period that corresponded with the release of an online database that included those news titles. While OCR correction can significantly improve the quality of the textual data, completely clean and accurate textual data is a practical impossibility. As a result, a related area of study in computer science and information retrieval examines how to create accurate classification models on text data that contains OCR errors.^[G@Moshi2010; @Packer2010; @Taghva2004.] One of the challenges of OCR errors for information retrieval tasks is that, while simpler tasks, such as a boolean search (which evaluates matches for particular word patterns according to the logic of the request), are generally resilient in the face of messy texts, more complex tasks, such as ones based on probability or modeling, degrade when operated on messy data. In their 2004 study of the effect of OCR errors on information retrieval tasks, Kazem Taghva et. al. compared a number of retrieval systems using OCR generated text and text that had been cleaned. They found that the tendency for OCR transcription to produce texts with significantly more words than the ground-truth documents negatively affected search results in systems that weighted the results based on the prevalence of a term in a document, such as those that used tf-idf (term frequency - inverse document frequency) scores.^[@Taghva2004, 2.] However, in the same study the authors determined that, while OCR cleaning improved overall performance, the presence of errors in documents being categorized could have little negative impact on classification tasks if "good quality OCR documents" were used "for training" of the classifier.^[@Taghva2004, 5.] In avoiding error-ridden documents in the creation the classification model, they excluded those errors from the language model, and as a result, the errors in future documents would be ignored, rather than used as evidence in classification. Based on these findings, I pursued a similar two step approach to the Seventh-day Adventist corpus, first cleaning the text to remove regularly occurring transcription errors and some programmatic corrections, and then using a subset of the "better quality" documents as the training data for the models.
