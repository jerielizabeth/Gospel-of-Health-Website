Reading Religious Culture through Topic Models

Topic modeling is an umbrella term for a variety of different algorithmic approaches to summarizing documents by identifying words that tend to appear together. While the mathematical details vary, these approaches take as inputs a collection of documents and a desired number of topics, and then use that information to assign the words in a document to the various topics, updating those assignments based on the probability of words occurring together across all of the documents.4 The algorithms produce two primary outputs: a list of words and their prevalence in each topic, and a list of topics and their prevalence in a document.

Of the different approaches to topic modeling, latent Dirichlet allocation (LDA), first developed in 2003, has become widely used and widely extended.5 LDA provides a model of topics present in a document and the words present in a topic, according to the assumption that individual documents are about relatively few topics and that topics are, on the whole, composed of relatively few words. The LDA parameters result in models where words can belong to multiple topics, and where documents are composed of a small set of topics. This is a representation of one way language works in written documents. There are certain words that are frequently used together when one is writing about sports, or zoology, or theology. In addition, documents tend to be focused on a small number of subject areas. Blei and others used those features of writing and language use to capture those words that occur together in a text and use that collection as a description of documents and corpora.

As researchers have applied LDA in multiple contexts and for a growing number of research questions, the algorithm has also been extended to model aspects such as authorial distinctiveness, change over time, and correlation between topics.6 By adding parameters to emphasize relationships between elements into the algorithm, such as between topics, between authors and their works, and between time and word usage, these extensions of LDA can quickly and powerfully model a range of factors that shape the development of texts. Of these, a correlated topic model, which identifies topics that tend to occur together, and a multiple-corpus model, which enables the creation of an overall model while capturing the distinctiveness of the constituent document collections, are two approaches which would likely yield productive results in exploring the literature of the Seventh-day Adventist denomination.

However, while these more specialized applications of LDA provide tools for exploring particular aspects of a corpus, my goal for this dissertation is to establish a baseline of sorts for using topic modeling in studying the development of religious movements. As the literary form produced by the early Seventh-day Adventists differs from the materials typically used to train topic modeling algorithms, and their cultural context is markedly different from that of the academic and encyclopedic literature typically used to develop and train the algorithms. This is not to claim that the the algorithms are not robust enough to accommodate those differences, just that it is useful to establish how the materials and the algorithm interact before applying algorithms that assume the contours of the very change I am setting out to explore.7

Looking from the fast-paced world of natural language processing, where new methods are continually expanding the ways we can computationally explore text, it is perhaps jarring to turn back to topic modeling. While the method spurred a flurry of blog posts and digital humanities projects in the years around 2010, in the time since newer approaches, such as word embeddings, and different questions, such as text reuse, have dominated the scholarly landscape.8 These approaches are proving quite powerful, enabling scholars to explore focused questions around word use and language shifts with word embeddings to publication networks, information dissemination and the history of ideas.

Topic modeling does, however, offer some significant advantages for the types of questions often pursued in historical research. Developed in response to problems of information retrieval, topic modeling is a form of distant reading focused on capturing the broad themes and overall contours of a large collection of documents. Significantly, it does not require human readers to categorize the documents or the define the themes ahead of the analysis. This has two advantages. First, it makes it possible to work on large collections of relatively unknown textual content. And second, it creates opportunities to capture “unexpected” themes and trends. Where a researcher who relies on manual categorization may have to process through the documents multiple times as new categories become apparent, topic modeling algorithms use word frequency information to capture the assigned number of topics, independent of which topics a researcher is interested in.

The degree of distance that topic modeling provides is also well suited for historical research. The algorithm focuses on word co-occurrences within documents, with “document” defined by the researcher. As a result, the “topics” offer document-level descriptive information, which provides an overview of the “types of things people talked about” in a given set of documents, while also pointing back to the original documents for further study on topics of interest. The “bag-of-words” representation used by topic modeling, where the algorithm considers the document to be a unordered collection of words and their counts, is both a strength and a weakness of the approach. Although this representation reduces the direct contextual information about word use, a loss that is costly for explorations of word meanings and associations, it is much less costly at the level of document description. When functioning as an index of sorts, the data from topic models directs researchers to documents of interest. At the same time, the model itself offers information about the general contours of the document collection, and when combined with the document metadata, can be used to indicate when and where particular topics were prevalent, and can be used to suggest related topics.

While a powerful algorithm for summarizing large collections of documents and guiding research questions and further reading, topics models can be difficult to verify and interpret, making them an easier algorithm than many to “lie” with. The strength of the algorithm is that it can illuminate “latent,” hidden, patterns or topics, but it is also possible for those topics to be noisy, to include words that don’t fit or only fit due to coincidence. In addition, the algorithm will organize the words of the corpus into as many topics as the user designates, and the resulting model can appear “readable” at different numbers of topics.9 As a result, while the resulting models are useful, it is important to remember that they are abstractions of patterns in word co-occurrences and, like all models, are wrong in certain aspects.

In anticipation of some of the challenges of interpreting topic models , I have paid close attention to the creation of the model, including my work to select and preprocess the documents, and steps to verify the model.

The Structure of A Gospel of Health and Salvation

I have designed the project site to be read differently than a traditional historical narrative. At the core of the project is the browser of the topic model of the SDA literature, accessible at http://browser.dissertation.jeriwieringa.com. Contextualizing and interpreting this browser are five essays. The first provides an overview of Seventh-day Adventism in light of nineteenth-century religion and culture. This chapter is aimed at the reader who is unfamiliar with the context of American religious history or the particulars of the SDA, providing the necessary context for the interpretations forwarded throughout the rest of the dissertation. The second chapter provides methodological statements for the sources, processing, modeling, and interpretive aspects of the project. This chapter is the most technical in content, while also providing the framing links tying the computational work to the historical interpretation. The third chapter uses the topic model to identify four major cycles of end-times expectation during the first seventy years of the denomination’s history. Tracing the prominence of end-times related topics throughout the periodical literature of the denomination, the chapter bridges the computational model and more traditional historical research into the development of the particular religious culture of Seventh-day Adventism. The four and fifth chapters discuss the religious culture within these four major cycles, with chapter four examining denominational members’ early internal focus on belief and behavior and chapter five examining the later external focus on missions and [?].

As a project based on the computational analysis of text, a large portion of the intellectual and scholarly work the make up the dissertation exists in code. In considering the methodological processes around the use of computational analysis in history, this dissertation makes the claim, both explicitly and implicitly, that this “technical” work is of central importance to the scholarly object of the dissertation. Where traditional methods of review rely on the use of clear and well formatted footnotes so that the trail of evidence can be retraced and evaluated, the review of computational driven work requires the use of documented and accessible code, so that both the execution of the techniques and the assumptions embedded within the technical choices, can be seen and evaluated. To provide this documentation, the dissertation also includes a series of Jupyter Notebooks](/notebooks/) used to execute the computational work of the dissertation, along with a Python library and documentation. These code elements can be viewed through the dissertation’s web interface, or can be downloaded and run either for verification or for adaptation to other projects. This work of making the technical elements of the dissertation visible and extensible is key to establishing processes for validating computationally-based scholarship in the humanities, an extension of the push for reproducible research in the sciences.

The final elements of the dissertation provide documentation for the different components of the projects, including the final website, the model browser, and the dissertation sources. Per the requirements of the Department of History and Art History at George Mason University, the project includes a process statement, which provides an account of the methods and technologies used in the creation of the dissertation. Traditional documentation elements, such as bibliography and acknowledgments, are also included in the “About” section. Together, all of these aspects of the project constitute the body of work that is A Gospel of Health and Salvation.

While the dissertation itself is the composite of these different computational and interpretive elements, readers do not need to be experts in all aspects of the project in order to read and evaluate the dissertation. The essays outline my approach and my intellectual decision in structuring and carrying out the computational analysis, providing an overview of the work without requiring the reader to directly engage the computational aspects of the work. Including the code enables readers to evaluate how well I have implemented those ideas and are included as part of a commitment to reproducible research. The concept of reproducible research, while increasingly prevalent in the sciences, is less common in the humanities. It refers to the goal of sufficiently describing ones computational methods and process so that others can reproduce the results when given the same data.10 Although the emphasis on code and computation is new, the concept of making historical research verifiable is not, as historians have long incorporated methods for verification in their academic writing through the mechanism of footnotes.11 By releasing the code and describing the processes used, scholars who rely on computational methods provide the information necessary for that work to be recognized and critically engaged, and in so doing make it part of the scholarly conversation.

Conclusions

A Note on Religion

As with other areas of history where there is an active community with a vested interest in the history told, it is common for scholars of religion to state their own religious history and affiliation. For the Seventh-day Adventist community, the literature about the denomination has, to date, tended to fall into two general categories: sympathetic histories by Seventh-day Adventist authors and critical histories by authors external to or critical of the faith.12 This has led to some concerns when new faces appear asking questions, concerns politely expressed to me as I started visiting Seventh-day Adventists archives and research centers.

My approach to the study of religion fits somewhere on the edges of the “Open Critical History.” Along with many other historians of religion, I am interested in religious movements because I find religion and religious belief to be an irreducible category of analysis - something that shapes and explains the behavior of individuals and the development of groups, and that cannot be explained away through reference to economics or other social pressures.

I came to the study of Seventh-day Adventism as a sympathetic outsider - as someone raised in the small, historically Dutch, Christian Reformed denomination and brought up within the American evangelical subculture. Seventh-day Adventism was not a religious group I was deeply aware of prior to beginning this study, and the process of learning to develop an understanding of a tradition far outside my own has been a transformational one. While I am sure that I have missed some of the details and nuances of the beliefs and structures I describe here, I have attempted to sketch the overall contours of the patterns I found and the ways those changed over my period of study.
