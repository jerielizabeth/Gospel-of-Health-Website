Jeri E. Wieringa

2018-02-01 00:00

Source

[anecdote to start]

In this dissertation, I examine the unique religious culture that developed during the first eighty years of Seventh-day Adventism. I argue that cycles of end-times expectation and the religious leadership of Ellen White contributed to the formation of a religious culture marked by domestic authority, cooperative organization, and the activism of women, seen in the movement’s religious understanding and pursuit of health. This culture provides an instructive counterpoint to accounts of nineteenth-century revivalism that stress the reinforcement of patriarchal structures and capitalistic economic structures in the aftermath of the revivals of the nineteenth century.

Women and the Revivals of the Early Republic

As will be discussed in the first essay, the early nineteenth-century was a period in American history marked by religious innovation. The separation of religion from state control, a process known as disestablishment, created an environment particularly ripe for a “democratization” of religious expression.1 People were increasingly able to worship according to their preference and to establish churches outside of the control of the Anglican (Episcopal) church in the South and the Congregational church in New England. As a result of disestablishment, the Baptist and Methodist traditions, which had existed both in the colonies and in Britain and Europe prior to the Revolution, flourished in the American context. Membership in Methodist churches grew to 250,000 by 1820 and 500,000 by 1830, nearly four percent of the population, and Baptists saw a similar increase, with membership growing “tenfold” in the decades after the Revolution.2 The loosening of social controls on religious expression also resulted in increasing diversity among religious traditions, as new churches, denominations, and movements formed in response to differences in religious thought and practice. Out of this period of religious innovation came some of the most distinctive American religious and social movements, including the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter Day Saints, the Oneida Utopian Community, and the Seventh-day Adventists.3

The decentralization of control, however, is not sufficient to explain the rapid proliferation of new religious movements, each claiming to present the truth that others were failing uphold or communicate. Looking more closely at religious revival and reform movements, it becomes clear that these religious actors were guided by particular visions of sacred history, by particular understandings of the arc of history and the role of American Protestant Christians in that narrative. While each group understood itself as standing at the crux of the story of salvation, all shared a framework that “began in Eden, developed in the world, and would culminate in a world without end.” The narrative was radically expansive, encompassing “humankind – saints and sinners, men and women, common and chosen …” along with “the suprahuman character of God Himself, His son the Christ, and the sworn enemy – the Antichrist.”4 In the march toward the last judgment, each group identified itself as the chosen one whose members successfully understood and obeyed the requirements for salvation, whether those requirements were correct behavior, correct attitude toward the divine, or the proper experience to ensure a connection with and the favor of the Divine. For many, and particularly for those traditions coming out of New England Puritanism, that position also required bringing about change in the world, either by converting souls through revival or by advocating for virtuous living. These various Protestant groups saw themselves as having a divine mission to prepare the world for the coming end and to do their part to ensure that events unfolded according to the Divine plan.

Protestant women as well as men embraced these various efforts to redeem the world ahead of the imminent return of Christ. As has been noted by Ann Braude and others, religion in the American context has always been a site of significant female activity.5 Although leadership positions have been restricted to male adherents across most of the American religious traditions, women have consistently constituted the majority of those who attend, who contribute both time and money to religious initiatives, and who work to train children within the religious tradition.6 Building on the existing predominance of religious women, the Protestant reform movements of the early nineteenth-century called upon women to help bring about revival and reform. As Carroll Smith-Rosenberg has noted, although these movements were generally led by “male clerics and reformers … women were their most zealous adherents.”7 In addition to joining radical religious movements, women worked for reform in a variety of ways, from fundraising for various causes to creating female benevolent societies to themselves taking to the pulpit and preaching to convince others to repent for the kingdom of heaven was at hand. Through all these efforts, women created “a public and powerful role for themselves as a female conscience and moral voice crying in a wilderness of male corruption” during the same period that domesticity and “the Cult of True Womanhood” grew in cultural prominence.8

Radical religious movements attracted the involvement of women and presented new opportunities for them to claim leadership roles that had been previously been denied. Sociologists and historians have linked periods of revival and radical religion to periods of social and economic change and distress, when “boundaries are no longer clearly defined.”9 Revivals, as “liminal” periods, provided space for the reconfiguration of social norms and structures, enabling women and others traditionally excluded from leadership roles to experiment with different constructions and in so doing, shift the shape of the social spheres.10 For the revivals of the Second Great Awakening, the social reorganization launched with the American Revolution and the instability of society brought about with increased mobility and the loosening of government control on the expression of religion created the environment in which groups traditional excluded - those without formal training in divinity, women, and ethnic minorities - could experiment with alternative structures of religious practice and qualification.

For the women drawn to radical religion, their involvement took a variety of forms, from active preaching to more gender-conforming support roles, and was explained in a variety of ways. While preaching and teaching in front of mix gender groups constituted in many ways the height of women’s break from the cultural norms, there were additional avenues open to those who did not directly feel the call to preach. From working within the growing outreach organizations of the revival movements as “social workers, teachers, managers, missionaries, and writers” to encouraging revivals “through home meeting, prayer groups, and voluntary and missions work,” women found a range of opportunities where they could contribute to the cause.11 The “social” space of religious life, positioned as it was between the “public” and “private,” between commerce or government and the family, provided a range of opportunities for women to apply their faith.12 In defending their assumption of more forward roles in the promotion of the faith, religious women deployed a variety of strategies to navigate the balance between their religious convictions and the religious and cultural constraints. For some, and particularly for adventists, Scripture itself could be invoked for support, especially Joel’s prophecy that in the last days God’s spirit would be poured out on both young men and women. As a result, “the sight of a woman standing in the pulpit had cosmic significance: she was a living symbol of the last days.”13 For others, they relied on what became a standard set of strategies for situating themselves and their calling within an acceptable religious framework. Nearly all in telling their stories inform their audience of their strong resistance to the call, and often of their attempts to avoid it. Eventually, however, they find that they cannot resist, and so appeal to the listeners sympathy in claiming that “the authority of God outweighs that of … parents, husbands, and friend, even that of their own conscience.”14 By separating their activity from “ambition” or personal desire, and presenting themselves as weak and (apparently) unwilling vessels, women within revival movements were able to find an unstable ground on which to claim some authority within revival movements.

Building a Computational Study of Religious Culture

[why using computational methods for this study. Framework within which those methods are deployed.]

Early Seventh-day Adventists, like their predecessors in the Millerite and Christian Connection movements, were highly prolific writers, relying on print publications to spread their message and win converts. As a result, even within just the periodical literature of the denomination, there is more written content than can be effectively managed for historical analysis. As a result, previous studies of Seventh-day Adventism have relied primarily on the major publication of the denomination, The Review and Herald, or relied on selective reading to explore different aspects or periods of the denomination’s history.

The development of computational methods for classifying texts offers a third way to approach the prolific periodical literature of the denomination.

The Structure of A Gospel of Health and Salvation

Works of historical scholarship typically take a narrative format, telling the story of the past and showing how various cultural forces and human actors shaped the development of historical events. However, the development of computational technologies creates the space and the need to also consider the processes by which historical interpretation takes place. While this dissertation includes elements of historical story-telling, my primary focus is on methodology, specifically, the incorporation of computational models of text into the work of historical analysis. As such, this dissertation is an applied methodological study organized around a historical research question: how does time, and the expectation of the second coming, shape the development of the religious culture of Seventh-day Adventism?

At the core of the project is the browser of the topic model of the SDA literature, accessible at http://browser.dissertation.jeriwieringa.com. This model, built using MALLET and visualized using Andrew Goldstone’s DfR Browser, provides a window onto the language used across the corpus of Seventh-day Adventist texts that I assembled. The interface is organized according to my interpretation of those topics, as reflected in the topic labels. I discuss my interpretive process for creating the labels in chapter two.

The work of selecting and preparing a corpus of text for topic modeling is one of interpretation, and sets the limits of possibility for the resulting computational model. As Supporting the model are the dissertation notebooks, which document the technical work that went into creating and interpreting the topic model.

The first essay, “The Emergence of Seventh-day Adventism,” provides an overview of Seventh-day Adventism in light of nineteenth-century religion and culture. This chapter is aimed at the reader who is unfamiliar with the context of American religious history or the particulars of the SDA, providing the necessary context for the interpretations forwarded throughout the rest of the dissertation.

The second essay, “Constructing Computational Models from Historical Texts,” provides methodological statements for the sources, processing, modeling, and interpretive aspects of the project. This chapter is the most technical in content, while also providing the framing links tying the computational work to the historical interpretation.

The third chapter, “Anticipating the End of Time,” establishes the problem of time within early Seventh-day Adventism and uses the topic model to identify four major cycles of end-times expectation during the first seventy years of the denomination’s history. A case study on using topic models to identify and reveal overarching trends in a corpus for historical analysis, the chapter Tracing the prominence of end-times related topics throughout the periodical literature of the denomination, the chapter bridges the computational model and more traditional historical research into the development of the particular religious culture of Seventh-day Adventism.

The forth and final chapter, “,” offers a second case study for the use of topic modeling and textual analysis for historical research, this time at the level of close reading. Where chapter 3 focused on broad patterns, chapter four explore in detail the gender dynamics in two prominent areas of Christian labor: home and missions.

As a project based on the computational analysis of text, a large portion of the intellectual and scholarly work the make up the dissertation exists in code. In considering the methodological processes around the use of computational analysis in history, this dissertation makes the claim, both explicitly and implicitly, that this “technical” work is of central importance to the scholarly object of the dissertation. Where traditional methods of review rely on the use of clear and well formatted footnotes so that the trail of evidence can be retraced and evaluated, the review of computational driven work requires the use of documented and accessible code, so that both the execution of the techniques and the assumptions embedded within the technical choices, can be seen and evaluated. The code elements can be viewed through the dissertation’s web interface, or can be downloaded and run either for verification or for adaptation to other projects. The work of making the technical elements of the dissertation visible and extensible is key to establishing processes for validating computationally-based scholarship in the humanities, an extension of the push for reproducible research in the sciences.

The final elements of the dissertation provide documentation for the different components of the projects, including the final website, the model browser, and the dissertation sources. Per the requirements of the Department of History and Art History at George Mason University, the project includes a process statement, which provides an account of the methods and technologies used in the creation of the dissertation. Traditional documentation elements, such as bibliography and acknowledgments, are also included in the “About” section. Together, all of these aspects of the project constitute the body of work that is A Gospel of Health and Salvation.

While the dissertation itself is the composite of these different computational and interpretive elements, readers do not need to be experts in all aspects of the project in order to read and evaluate the dissertation. The essays outline my approach and my intellectual decision in structuring and carrying out the computational analysis, providing an overview of the work without requiring the reader to directly engage the computational aspects of the work. Including the code enables readers to evaluate how well I have implemented those ideas and are included as part of a commitment to reproducible research. The concept of reproducible research, while increasingly prevalent in the sciences, is less common in the humanities. It refers to the goal of sufficiently describing ones computational methods and process so that others can reproduce the results when given the same data.15 Although the emphasis on code and computation is new, the concept of making historical research verifiable is not, as historians have long incorporated methods for verification in their academic writing through the mechanism of footnotes.16 By releasing the code and describing the processes used, scholars who rely on computational methods provide the information necessary for that work to be recognized and critically engaged, and in so doing make it part of the scholarly conversation.
