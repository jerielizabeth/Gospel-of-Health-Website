How to Read A Gospel of Health and Salvation

I have designed the project site to be read differently than a traditional historical narrative. At the core of the project is the browser of the topic model of the SDA literature, accessible at http://browser.dissertation.jeriwieringa.com. The browser exposes a number of metadata aspects so that readers can explore how different factors, such as location, periodical type, or year, affect the general patterns that the model describes. Surrounding this browser are four essays, the first two provide context for the content and computational work involved, while the second two use the model to offer an account of the development of Seventh-day Adventism’s religious culture. In addition, the project includes a series of Jupyter Notebooks that I used to execute the computational work of the dissertation, along with a Python library and documentation so that the technical work can be examined, verified, and modified for other projects. Finally, per the requirements of the Department of History and Art History at George Mason University, the project includes a process statement, along with a traditional bibliography, included in the “About” section.

The various layers of the project offer different things to different types of readers, from those curious about the history of Seventh-day Adventism to those interested in computational methods in historical research. For readers less interested in the technical work, the essays provide a more traditional historical narrative and a particular interpretation of the patterns created by the topic model, with sufficient context to evaluate the persuasiveness of the arguments. For readers who wish to dig deeper into the model and into the intellectual choices behind it, the model browser and the code documented in the Notebooks and the Python library offer the details of my work. Finally, for readers who wish to use the code to verify the work here or adapt it for their own research, the notebooks and the python library can be downloaded and installed locally to test the methods or modify them for use on other data sets.

Together, all of these aspects of the project constitute the body of work that is A Gospel of Health and Salvation. At the same time, readers do not need to be experts in all aspects of the project in order to evaluate the dissertation. The essays outline my approach and my intellectual decision in structuring and carrying out the computational analysis, providing an overview of the work without requiring the reader to directly engage the computational aspects of the work. Including the code enables readers to evaluate how well I have implemented those ideas and are included as part of a commitment to reproducible research. The concept of reproducible research, while increasingly prevalent in the sciences, is less common in the humanities. It refers to the goal of sufficiently describing ones computational methods and process so that others can reproduce the results when given the same data.1 Although the emphasis on code and computation is new, the concept of making historical research verifiable is not, as historians have long incorporated methods for verification in their academic writing through the mechanism of footnotes.2 By releasing the code and describing the processes used, scholars who rely on computational methods provide the information necessary for that work to be recognized and critically engaged, and in so doing make it part of the scholarly conversation.
